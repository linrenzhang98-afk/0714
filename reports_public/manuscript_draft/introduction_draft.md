# Introduction draft

Shotgun metagenomic studies of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) are becoming increasingly available across pulmonary disease settings. Their joint interpretation nevertheless remains limited by differences in preprocessing, taxonomic databases, assignment definitions, feature filters and ecological representations. Apparent differences between studies can therefore reflect measurement as well as clinical or biological heterogeneity. Cross-study analysis should define what is measured consistently without treating technical alignment as proof that populations are interchangeable.

Community-level ecological structure offers one useful level of inquiry. Individual taxon findings can be sensitive to sparsity, database coverage, contamination and multiplicity, whereas whole-community geometry asks how relative organization varies across prespecified clinical groups. This does not make community metrics universally transportable, but it permits effect-size and dispersion questions to be posed consistently. In particular, compositional analysis separates relative organization within the observed feature set from classifier yield, which is better treated as a technical endpoint.

Here, a common native Kraken2 classifier-assignment layer applies the same database, parameters, report structure and direct-assignment definition to two BALF shotgun cohorts. The layer standardizes what is counted; it does not erase differences in source processing, read architecture, recruitment or clinical labels. Direct species assignments remain a classifier-defined subcomposition, and classified fraction does not measure bacterial biomass.

We therefore designed a two-resolution pulmonary ecology study. In the 400-patient PRJNA1056765 anchor cohort, we estimate the between-diagnosis association of four published pulmonary diagnoses with BALF species composition. In the 130-subject PRJCA046985 cohort, we separately estimate within-TB community differences between Drug_Resistance and Drug_Sensitive groups. The cohorts are connected by a common classifier-defined measurement layer and a prespecified robustness framework, not by a shared clinical effect.

We define ecological generalizability operationally: the same measurement definition must be applicable without cohort-specific redesign; each cohort must yield interpretable contrast-specific effect and dispersion estimates; representation dependence must be reported rather than selected away; and common observability and technical limitations may be compared. This definition does not imply a common direction, coefficient, taxon set, disease signature or mechanism.
